Literature Analysis Summary Packet

Holes, Chapters 19-29 — teacher summaries, student-reader summaries, and board words.

Volunteer reader routine: Assign one student to each chapter. The student writes the chapter's board word, reads the summary aloud, and then the class answers one question. The board words can be reused in the word search.

Holes — Chapters 19-29

Chapter 19

Teacher — 1 sentence

Stanley is blamed for the sunflower seeds after Magnet steals them.

Student reader — board word: SEEDS
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

7th-grade read-aloud

Chapter 19 begins with a small act of rule-breaking that quickly becomes a serious problem. Mr. Sir has a sack of sunflower seeds, and the boys know they are not supposed to have them. Magnet steals the sack from Mr. Sir's truck and tosses it around to the other boys. For a moment, the stolen seeds feel like a treat and a joke, but the mood changes when the sack lands in Stanley's hole and spills everywhere.

Stanley is trapped in a difficult position. He did not steal the seeds, but the evidence is sitting in his hole. If he tells the truth, he would have to point to Magnet and the others. At Camp Green Lake, loyalty matters because the boys have very little protection from the adults. Stanley decides to take the blame, partly because he is trying to survive socially and partly because he does not want to betray the group. Mr. Sir is furious and takes Stanley to the Warden. This chapter shows how quickly a small choice can become dangerous in a place where the adults already have power and the boys are always trying to protect themselves.

As you listen, notice that Stanley's choice is not simple. He is not just being honest or dishonest. He is weighing danger from Mr. Sir against danger from the other boys. The chapter asks readers to think about what people do when every option has a cost.

QuestionListen For
Why does Stanley take the blame for the seeds?He wants to protect Magnet and avoid turning against the group, even though it makes Mr. Sir angry at him.
What does this event show about the pressure among the boys?The boys are under pressure to stay loyal to each other, even when someone else caused the problem.
How does Stanley's choice affect Mr. Sir's attitude toward him?Mr. Sir becomes more hostile toward Stanley and takes him to the Warden.

Chapter 20

Teacher — 1 sentence

The Warden punishes Mr. Sir instead of Stanley, showing her frightening control.

Student reader — board word: VENOM
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

7th-grade read-aloud

Chapter 20 takes Stanley inside the Warden's world again, and the scene makes it clear that she controls everyone at Camp Green Lake. Mr. Sir brings Stanley to her because he expects Stanley to be punished for the sunflower seeds. Stanley is nervous because he has already seen that the Warden is not a normal authority figure. She is calm on the outside, but that calmness makes her more frightening, not less.

Instead of simply punishing Stanley, the Warden turns her anger toward Mr. Sir. She uses her fingernails, which are painted with rattlesnake venom, to hurt him. The punishment is shocking because Mr. Sir is one of the adults who usually frightens the boys. In this scene, he becomes helpless in front of the Warden. Stanley learns something important: the camp does not operate by fairness or justice. It operates by fear. The Warden can punish a camper, a counselor, or anyone else whenever she chooses. For readers, the chapter raises the stakes because Stanley is not only trapped in a harsh camp; he is trapped under a leader who is dangerous, secretive, and unpredictable.

This scene also changes how we understand Mr. Sir. He is cruel to the boys, but he is not the most powerful person at the camp. The Warden's power reaches downward through everyone. That power structure helps explain why Camp Green Lake feels so unsafe.

QuestionListen For
Why does Mr. Sir take Stanley to the Warden?He wants Stanley punished for the sunflower seeds and thinks the Warden will side with him.
How does the Warden surprise both Mr. Sir and Stanley?She hurts Mr. Sir with her venom nail polish instead of punishing Stanley.
What does this chapter show about power at Camp Green Lake?The Warden has frightening power over everyone, including the counselors.

Chapter 21

Teacher — 1 sentence

Stanley thinks about God's Thumb and survival in the desert.

Student reader — board word: THUMB
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

7th-grade read-aloud

In Chapter 21, Stanley returns from the Warden's cabin and goes back to the exhausting work of digging. The chapter slows down and lets us sit with Stanley's thoughts. He has just watched the Warden hurt Mr. Sir, and he knows Mr. Sir will probably blame him even though Stanley did not cause the Warden's cruelty. Stanley is beginning to understand that at Camp Green Lake, doing the right thing does not always protect you.

As he digs, Stanley thinks about his family history, especially the story of his great-grandfather. According to the family story, Stanley's great-grandfather was once robbed and left in the desert, but he survived after finding refuge on a place called God's Thumb. This memory is important because it connects Stanley's present situation to an older story of danger and survival. He is also in a desert. He is also powerless. He is also trying to stay alive in a place that seems designed to defeat him. The chapter encourages readers to notice the physical setting: the dry lake bed, the distant landforms, and the history buried in the desert. It hints that Stanley's family stories may matter more than they first seemed.

At this point, the book is training readers to pay attention to repeated images and stories. The thumb-shaped place, the desert, and the family curse may sound like separate details, but they are starting to echo each other. Stanley does not fully understand the pattern yet, but readers can begin watching for it.

QuestionListen For
What old family story does Stanley remember?He remembers that his great-grandfather survived in the desert after finding refuge on God's Thumb.
Why might God's Thumb matter in this part of the book?It may connect Stanley's family history to the land around Camp Green Lake.
How does the setting connect the past and present?The desert, mountain, and old family story begin to feel linked to Stanley's current situation.

Chapter 22

Teacher — 1 sentence

Stanley and Zero make a deal: reading lessons for digging help.

Student reader — board word: TEACH
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

7th-grade read-aloud

Chapter 22 begins an important change in Stanley's relationship with Zero. Until now, many characters have treated Zero as if he is empty, silent, or unintelligent. Even his nickname reduces him to nothing. But Zero approaches Stanley with a specific request: he wants Stanley to teach him how to read. This moment matters because Zero is not asking for pity. He is asking to learn, and he is willing to work for it.

Stanley agrees, and Zero offers to dig part of Stanley's hole in exchange for reading lessons. On the surface, this looks like a trade: education for labor. But underneath, something deeper is happening. Stanley begins to see Zero as a person with goals, effort, and intelligence. Zero begins to trust Stanley enough to admit something private. Their arrangement also creates tension because the camp is built around punishment and competition, not cooperation. If one boy gets help, the others may see it as unfair. The deal between Stanley and Zero becomes the beginning of a friendship, but it also sets up conflict with the other boys. This chapter is a good place to discuss point of view because readers understand Zero more clearly as Stanley starts paying real attention to him.

Another important idea is dignity. Zero has been mocked and dismissed, but learning to read is one way he can claim part of himself back. Stanley also changes because teaching Zero gives him a role besides victim or prisoner. They both become more human to each other.

QuestionListen For
Why does Zero want Stanley to teach him?Zero cannot read and wants to learn, even though others underestimate him.
Why does Stanley agree?Zero offers to dig part of Stanley's hole, and Stanley begins to see Zero differently.
How does this deal change their relationship?They move from barely speaking to helping each other, beginning a real friendship.

Chapter 23

Teacher — 1 sentence

The book begins the Green Lake past timeline with Katherine and Sam.

Student reader — board word: SCHOOL
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

7th-grade read-aloud

Chapter 23 shifts away from Stanley's present and takes readers into the past. This is one of the clearest examples of the novel's multiple timelines. Instead of the dry, empty Camp Green Lake, we see Green Lake as it used to be: a real lake town with water, people, a schoolhouse, and daily life. The contrast is important. The same place that now feels dead and punishing once had a community.

In this past timeline, Miss Katherine Barlow is the schoolteacher. She is respected and known for her kindness and her famous spiced peaches. Sam is an onion seller who travels by boat and is skilled at fixing things. He and his donkey, Mary Lou, are familiar parts of the town. The chapter also introduces Trout Walker, a wealthy and arrogant man who wants Katherine's attention. Katherine is not interested in him, which bruises his pride. At first, the old Green Lake setting may seem peaceful compared with the camp, but the chapter plants the seeds of future conflict. By moving into the past, the author starts explaining how the land, the treasure, and the suffering at Camp Green Lake are connected to older choices and injustices.

For readers, this chapter is like opening a hidden layer underneath the setting. Camp Green Lake is not just a weird name for a camp with no lake. It is a place that has changed dramatically. The question becomes: what happened here, and why is Stanley digging in this exact place?

QuestionListen For
How is old Green Lake different from Camp Green Lake?Old Green Lake had water, a town, a school, and ordinary life; Camp Green Lake is dry and punishing.
Who are Katherine Barlow and Sam?Katherine is the schoolteacher, and Sam sells onions and helps repair things.
Why does the author move into the past here?The past timeline explains the place, the mystery, and the buried history readers need.

Chapter 24

Teacher — 1 sentence

Mr. Sir punishes Stanley by withholding water.

Student reader — board word: WATER
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

7th-grade read-aloud

Chapter 24 returns to the present timeline at Camp Green Lake, where Stanley is still dealing with the consequences of the sunflower seed incident. Mr. Sir was humiliated and hurt by the Warden, but he cannot fight back against her. Instead, he takes his anger out on Stanley. This is an important power pattern in the book: someone with less power suffers because someone above them is cruel, and then that person passes the cruelty downward.

Water becomes the main symbol of control in this chapter. In the desert, water is not a luxury. It is survival. When Mr. Sir refuses to give Stanley his fair amount of water, the punishment is not just uncomfortable; it is dangerous. Stanley understands that complaining could make things worse. He has learned that the adults at Camp Green Lake do not have to be fair, and no one outside the camp is watching closely enough to protect him. This chapter helps readers feel the physical reality of the setting: heat, thirst, dust, and exhaustion. It also shows how the present-day camp uses basic needs, like water and rest, as tools of power. Stanley's silence is not weakness; it is a survival choice in an unfair system.

This is also a good chapter for cause and effect. Stanley took the blame for the seeds, the Warden punished Mr. Sir, and now Mr. Sir punishes Stanley. No single event stays isolated. Each action creates another reaction, which is part of why the camp feels impossible to escape.

QuestionListen For
Why is Mr. Sir angry at Stanley?He blames Stanley for what happened with the Warden and the sunflower seeds.
Why is water such a serious punishment in this setting?The camp is dangerously hot and dry, so withholding water is cruel and dangerous.
What does Stanley's silence show?It shows he feels powerless and knows complaining may make things worse.

Chapter 25

Teacher — 1 sentence

Katherine and Sam grow closer through the schoolhouse repairs and onion visits.

Student reader — board word: ONIONS
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

7th-grade read-aloud

Chapter 25 stays in the past and develops the relationship between Katherine and Sam. Sam sells onions and also knows how to repair things, so Katherine keeps finding reasons to ask for his help at the schoolhouse. Their conversations grow warmer and more personal. Sam is patient, practical, and kind. Katherine respects him, and their friendship becomes love.

The problem is that their town is shaped by racism. Katherine is a white woman, and Sam is a Black man. Even though their relationship is loving and mutual, the community around them does not see it that way. The chapter shows how prejudice can turn ordinary happiness into danger. The lake town still has beauty, water, peaches, school, and community, but now readers see the ugliness underneath. When Katherine and Sam kiss, the moment should be tender, but it becomes frightening because someone sees them. This is a major turning point in the past timeline. It helps explain why the old Green Lake story is not just background information. The pain and injustice from the past are part of what shaped the land and the mystery Stanley is living inside in the present.

Students should notice the contrast between private character and public judgment. Sam is shown as generous and capable, and Katherine is shown as thoughtful and brave. But the town judges them through prejudice instead of character. That contrast helps the chapter feel unfair before the violence even begins.

QuestionListen For
How does Sam help Katherine?He sells onions and repairs the schoolhouse, giving them time to talk and know each other.
What feelings begin to grow between Katherine and Sam?They begin to love each other.
Why is their relationship dangerous in their town?The racist town rejects a relationship between a white woman and a Black man.

Chapter 26

Teacher — 1 sentence

Katherine Barlow becomes Kissin' Kate Barlow after Sam is killed.

Student reader — board word: OUTLAW
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

7th-grade read-aloud

Chapter 26 is one of the darkest chapters in this section because the danger in the past timeline becomes violence. After Katherine and Sam's relationship is discovered, the town turns against them. The schoolhouse is destroyed, and the people who should protect justice do not protect them. The sheriff is corrupt and cruel, and the town's racism gives the mob permission to act violently.

Sam tries to escape across the lake with Mary Lou, but he is killed. Katherine survives, but the loss changes her life completely. Her grief is mixed with rage because Sam's death is not an accident. It is the result of hatred and injustice. After this, Katherine Barlow becomes Kissin' Kate Barlow, an outlaw who robs men and leaves behind her famous kiss. This transformation does not excuse everything she later does, but it helps readers understand how she became that person. The chapter also changes how we see Green Lake. The dry lake bed is not only a strange setting; it is a place with buried violence and unresolved history. The past timeline gives emotional weight to the present timeline because Stanley is digging in ground connected to these events.

This chapter also helps explain the novel's moral complexity. Kate becomes an outlaw, but the book first shows readers the injustice that broke her life apart. The author is asking us to think about how people are shaped by what happens to them, especially when society itself is unjust.

QuestionListen For
What happens to Sam?A mob kills Sam after his relationship with Katherine is discovered.
How does this tragedy change Katherine?Her grief and anger help turn her into the outlaw Kissin' Kate Barlow.
How does this past event change the reader's understanding of Camp Green Lake?Readers see that the dry lake bed has a history of love, racism, violence, and buried pain.

Chapter 27

Teacher — 1 sentence

Zero's reading lessons continue, and Stanley learns Zero is strong in math.

Student reader — board word: MATH
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

7th-grade read-aloud

Chapter 27 returns to Stanley and Zero in the present. Their reading lessons continue, and the chapter pushes readers to question the label everyone has placed on Zero. The other boys call him Zero because they think there is nothing inside him, but Stanley begins to see that this is false. Zero may struggle with reading, but he is quick with numbers and pays close attention. His problem is not a lack of intelligence. His problem is that no one has taken the time to teach him.

The trade between Stanley and Zero also begins to cause more tension in the group. Zero digs part of Stanley's hole, which helps Stanley physically, but the other boys see the arrangement as unfair. Their resentment grows because every boy at Camp Green Lake is exhausted, thirsty, and competing for small advantages. The chapter is important for point of view because readers mostly understand Zero through Stanley's changing perspective. As Stanley's view of Zero becomes more respectful, our view changes too. The chapter also connects to the larger theme of names and labels. A nickname like Zero can hide who someone really is, but Stanley is beginning to look past the label.

Readers should also watch Stanley's growth. At the beginning of the book, Stanley mostly worried about surviving his own hole. Now he is becoming someone who can help another person. That does not make camp easy, but it shows Stanley gaining moral strength.

QuestionListen For
What does Stanley learn about Zero?Zero is not stupid; he is determined, observant, and very good at math.
Why do the other boys resent the deal?They think Zero is doing Stanley's work and giving Stanley an unfair advantage.
How does point of view affect how we understand Zero?Readers see more of Zero's abilities as Stanley gets to know him, while other boys still judge him.

Chapter 28

Teacher — 1 sentence

Kissin' Kate Barlow connects Stanley's family history to the buried treasure mystery.

Student reader — board word: KATE
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

7th-grade read-aloud

Chapter 28 deepens the connection between the past timeline and Stanley's family history. After Katherine becomes Kissin' Kate Barlow, she becomes a famous outlaw. One of the people she robs is Stanley's great-grandfather. This is important because it means the old Green Lake story is not separate from Stanley's life. His family curse, his great-grandfather's survival story, and the mystery of Camp Green Lake are beginning to connect.

The chapter later shows Kate as an older woman returning to the dried-up lake. Trout Walker and Linda Miller, who have aged and become desperate, want her hidden treasure. They try to force her to reveal where it is buried. Kate refuses. When a yellow-spotted lizard bites her, she dies laughing and takes the secret with her. This ending makes the treasure mystery stronger because now readers know there really was stolen loot, and it may still be buried somewhere near the camp. The chapter also explains why the Warden might care so much about digging holes. Readers can begin to infer that the digging is not really about building character. It may be about searching for Kate Barlow's buried treasure.

This is a strong chapter for inference. The book does not need to announce every connection directly. Instead, readers collect clues: Kate had treasure, the treasure was never found, the lake dried up, and the Warden makes boys dig all day. Those details point toward a hidden purpose.

QuestionListen For
How is Kate connected to Stanley's family?She robbed Stanley's great-grandfather and left him in the desert.
What do Trout and Linda want from Kate?They want her to reveal where she buried her stolen treasure.
Why does Kate's ending deepen the treasure mystery?She dies without telling anyone where the treasure is, leaving the secret buried.

Chapter 29

Teacher — 1 sentence

A storm and a thumb-shaped mountain help Stanley connect old stories to the present.

Student reader — board word: STORM
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

7th-grade read-aloud

Chapter 29 brings the present timeline, the family-history timeline, and the old Green Lake timeline closer together. Back at Camp Green Lake, the weather changes. Clouds gather, thunder rolls, and lightning flashes in the distance. In a place that is usually dry and brutally hot, the possibility of a storm feels important. Even if rain does not immediately solve anything, the change in the sky makes the setting feel alive again.

Stanley notices a distant mountain formation that looks like a thumb. This image reminds him of the story of God's Thumb, the place where his great-grandfather supposedly found refuge after being robbed and left in the desert. Now that readers know Kissin' Kate Barlow robbed Stanley's great-grandfather, the story feels less like a random family legend and more like a clue. The landscape itself seems to be holding pieces of the past. This chapter is useful for discussing multiple timelines because the author is not simply jumping around for no reason. Each timeline gives readers a piece of the same puzzle. The camp, the old town, Kate Barlow, Stanley's family, and the distant mountain are all beginning to fit together.

The chapter also creates suspense. Stanley does not yet know what he will do with this information, and he does not know whether the mountain can help him. But readers can feel the story turning. The setting is no longer just a prison; it may contain a route toward truth and survival.

QuestionListen For
What does Stanley notice in the distance?He notices a mountain formation that looks like a thumb.
How does this connect to his great-grandfather's story?It reminds him of God's Thumb, where his great-grandfather survived.
Why is this chapter important for multiple timelines?The present, old Green Lake, and Stanley's family story begin connecting in one landscape.
