8th Grade First Day Literature Analysis

Book: Sarah, Plain and Tall by Patricia MacLachlan. First class focus: Chapter 1.

TAKE ATTENDANCE

Whiteboard Setup

Write this before students arrive.

Board Text How To Use It
Sarah, Plain and Tall - Chapter 1 Sets the focus text for the day.
Literature Analysis = What happened? + What does it show? + Why does it matter? Point to this whenever students only summarize.
Big Question: What is this family missing? Directs students toward grief, memory, family, and emotional conflict.
Characters: Anna, Caleb, Papa/Jacob, Mama, Sarah Helps students track who is present, remembered, or introduced through a letter.
Notice: singing, silence, memories, the advertisement, Sarah's letter Gives students concrete details to discuss if they struggle to begin.
Inference: Papa no longer sings, so we can infer ___. Pushes students beyond "he is sad" toward evidence-based interpretation.
Answer Frame: "I think ___ because ___." Trains students to support answers with evidence or reasons.

First Day Flow

Time What To Do Purpose
0-5 min Welcome students, introduce yourself briefly, and explain that literature analysis means asking deeper questions about characters, choices, conflict, and meaning. Set expectations without turning the first day into a lecture.
5-12 min Play Character Clue, described below. Learn names and build comfort speaking in a group.
12-17 min Introduce vocabulary: protagonist, setting, conflict, theme, inference, evidence. Give students words they can use immediately during discussion.
17-47 min Discuss Chapter 1 using the question bank. Practice moving from summary to analysis.
47-55 min Wrap up with: "What did we learn about a character from what they did, said, or remembered?" End with an analytical takeaway.

Icebreaker Game: Character Clue

How it works: Each student says their name and gives one "character clue" about themselves: something they like, something they are curious about, or a small problem they would want solved in a story. Example: "I'm Maya, and my character clue is that I always notice tiny details."

Bridge into literature: "Good readers do the same thing with characters. We notice clues: what people say, what they do, what they want, what they remember, and what problems they face."

Plan B: If Students Have Not Read Chapter 1

Use this if most or all students have not read yet. You can still teach inference, conflict, motivation, theme, and point of view using the opening situation.

What To Say

"Since this is our first meeting, some of you may not have had a chance to read Chapter 1 yet, and that is okay. Today we are going to practice the tools of literature analysis using the opening situation of Sarah, Plain and Tall."

"The story begins with a family living on the prairie: Anna, her younger brother Caleb, and their father, Jacob. Anna is the narrator. Their mother died years earlier, after Caleb was born, so Caleb does not really remember her. He asks Anna to tell him what their mother was like. Anna remembers that their mother used to sing, and she also notices that their father does not really sing anymore. That detail matters because it shows us that the family has not just lost a person; they have lost some of the warmth and joy that used to fill the house."

"Then their father tells Anna and Caleb that he has placed an advertisement for a wife. A woman named Sarah Wheaton, who lives in Maine by the sea, writes back. She describes herself as plain and tall. So the opening situation gives us a family shaped by grief, a father trying to solve a problem, two children hoping for something to change, and a stranger who might become part of their lives."

"The big question is not just, 'What happened?' The better question is, 'What does this opening reveal about what the family wants, fears, and needs?'"

Plan B Board Add-On

Board Text Use
Summary: What happened? Use this to separate summary from analysis.
Analysis: What does it reveal? Use this to push students toward inference and theme.
Hope vs. Fear Use this as the main Plan B activity.

Plan B Questions

Question Good Answer / Direction
What does this family seem to be missing? They are missing the mother, but also joy, singing, emotional warmth, and a sense of wholeness.
Why might Papa place an advertisement instead of simply staying as they are? He may need practical help, but he may also recognize that the family is lonely and incomplete.
What risks come with inviting a stranger into the family? Sarah might not fit, might not stay, or might remind them of what they lost. The family risks disappointment and more grief.
What might Anna and Caleb hope for? They may hope for a mother figure, companionship, a happier home, and a father who sings again.
What might Sarah risk by leaving Maine? She risks homesickness, loneliness, not belonging, and giving up the familiar sea for prairie life.
What themes could this story explore? Grief, family, hope, belonging, change, courage, memory, and the risk of loving someone new.

Plan B Mini Activity: Hope vs. Fear

  1. Draw two columns on the board: Hope and Fear.
  2. Use one row for each character: Anna, Caleb, Papa/Jacob, Sarah.
  3. Ask students what each person might hope for and fear.
  4. Strong examples: Anna hopes for family healing but fears disappointment. Caleb hopes for a mother figure but fears Sarah will not come or stay. Papa hopes for help and companionship but fears more loss. Sarah may hope for a new life but fear loneliness or not belonging.
  5. If time remains, discuss the title: Why might an author describe a character as "plain and tall"? Is "plain" necessarily negative?

Sarah, Plain and Tall Cheat Sheet

Whole-Book Plot Points

  • Anna narrates the story of her family on the prairie: her younger brother Caleb and their widowed father, Jacob Witting.
  • The family still feels the absence of Anna and Caleb's mother, who died after Caleb was born.
  • Jacob places a newspaper advertisement for a wife and mother.
  • Sarah Wheaton, from Maine, answers the advertisement and begins corresponding with the family.
  • Sarah agrees to visit the Witting family for a trial period before deciding whether to stay.
  • Anna and Caleb hope Sarah will become part of the family, but they worry she will miss the sea and return to Maine.
  • Sarah is practical, independent, plainspoken, and homesick, but she gradually becomes attached to the family and prairie life.
  • The emotional question of the book is not only "Will Sarah stay?" but "Can this family risk loving someone new after loss?"

Chapter 1 Summary

Chapter 1 introduces Anna, Caleb, their father Jacob, and the memory of Mama. Caleb asks Anna to tell him again what life was like when Mama was alive. Anna remembers singing in the house and knows that Papa no longer sings the way he once did. Caleb asks about his own birth, and Anna tells him what Mama said about him while quietly holding back the painful truth that Mama died after Caleb was born. Papa then tells the children he has placed an advertisement in the newspaper for a wife, and he receives a reply from Sarah Wheaton in Maine. Sarah describes herself plainly, including that she is plain and tall.

Answers To The Prep Questions

Question Good Answer
What does the family seem to be missing? They are missing Mama's presence, warmth, singing, and emotional center. They may also be missing a sense of wholeness as a family. Papa provides for them, but the household still carries grief and silence.
What do Anna and Caleb remember about their mother? Anna remembers more because she was older. She remembers Mama singing and the feeling of the house before Mama died. Caleb has no real memory of her, so he depends on Anna's stories to imagine her.
What question or feeling does Chapter 1 leave you with? Students may wonder whether Sarah will actually come, whether she will like the family, and whether Papa can become joyful again. The chapter leaves a feeling of hope mixed with uncertainty.

30-Minute Discussion Bank

Question Strong Answer / Direction
Who is the protagonist so far? Anna is the narrator and likely protagonist because we experience the story through her memories, worries, and observations.
What does Caleb want in Chapter 1? He wants to know his mother and understand the family story. He also wants Papa to sing again, which shows he wants joy restored to the home.
Why might Anna avoid telling Caleb everything about his birth? She loves him and does not want to burden him with guilt or pain. This shows Anna is protective and mature.
What does Papa's advertisement reveal about him? He is lonely and practical. He recognizes the family needs help, but the decision also suggests emotional need, not just household need.
What is the main conflict introduced? The family wants healing and a mother figure, but they face uncertainty: Sarah is a stranger, and no one knows whether she can belong with them.
How does the setting matter? The prairie setting can feel isolated, which makes the family's loneliness stronger. Sarah's home by the sea will later contrast with prairie life.
What does Sarah's description of herself suggest? She sounds honest and direct. Calling herself plain and tall suggests she is not trying to impress them falsely.
What is one theme beginning to appear? Possible themes: family, grief, hope, change, courage, belonging, and the risk of loving someone after loss.
What can we infer about Papa from the fact that he no longer sings? His grief changed him. The lost singing becomes a symbol of the family's lost joy.
How is analysis different from summary for this chapter? Summary says Papa places an ad and Sarah replies. Analysis asks why the ad matters, what it reveals about grief, and how the family's memories shape their hopes.

8th Grade Literature Vocabulary

Word Definition
ProtagonistThe main character or central character in a story.
AntagonistA character, force, or problem that works against the protagonist.
CharacterA person, animal, or being in a story.
SettingWhere and when a story takes place.
PlotThe sequence of important events in a story.
ConflictThe main problem or struggle in a story.
ThemeA deeper message or idea the story explores.
InferenceA smart conclusion based on clues in the text.
EvidenceDetails from the text that support an answer.
TraitA quality that describes a character, such as brave or selfish.
MotivationThe reason a character does something.
Point of ViewThe perspective from which a story is told.
ForeshadowingA hint that suggests something that may happen later.
SymbolAn object, action, or detail that represents a bigger idea.
ToneThe author's attitude or feeling in the writing.
MoodThe feeling a reader gets from a scene or story.
NarratorThe voice telling the story.
Internal ConflictA struggle inside a character's heart or mind.
External ConflictA struggle between a character and another person, society, nature, or circumstance.
CharacterizationThe way an author reveals what a character is like.
ImageryDescriptive language that helps readers imagine sights, sounds, smells, tastes, or feelings.
ContrastA difference between two characters, places, ideas, or situations.
ResolutionThe part of the story where major conflicts are settled.
SubtextMeaning that is implied but not directly stated.

Quick Teaching Reminders

  • Push one layer deeper: grief, memory, point of view, setting contrast, and inference.
  • Ask "What happened?" first only if students are lost. Then ask, "What does that show?" and "Why might the author include that?"
  • When students give a vague answer, ask: "What detail from the chapter makes you think that?"
  • Do not assign homework unless ISA or the parents tell you to. You can say, "Parents will guide reading expectations."
