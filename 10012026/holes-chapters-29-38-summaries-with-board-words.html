Literature Analysis Summary Packet

Holes, Chapters 29-38 — teacher summaries, student-reader summaries, and board words.

Volunteer reader routine: Assign one student to each chapter. The student writes the chapter's board word, reads the summary aloud, and then the class answers one question. The board words can be reused in the word search.

Holes — Chapters 29-38

Chapter 29

Teacher — 1 sentence

A storm near the thumb-shaped mountain helps Stanley connect his family story to the desert around Camp Green Lake.

Student reader — board word: THUMB
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

Read-aloud summary — about 2 minutes

Chapter 29 opens with a change in the weather. The boys see thunder and lightning far away from Camp Green Lake, but no rain reaches them. In a place where every day is dry, dusty, and brutally hot, even a storm in the distance feels important.

Stanley notices that one mountain looks like a giant fist with a thumb sticking up. That shape matters because it reminds him of the old family story about his great-grandfather surviving in the desert after finding refuge on God's Thumb. Earlier, the story may have sounded like a strange family legend. Now Stanley is standing in the same kind of desert, looking at a mountain that might fit the story.

This chapter is important because it links past and present. The family history, the land around the camp, and Stanley's present danger begin to point toward the same place. Stanley does not fully understand the connection yet, but readers can feel the story pulling together.

For discussion, this is a chapter where setting becomes evidence. The mountain is not only scenery; it may be a clue. Students should notice how Stanley's memory changes the way he sees the land. Before this, God's Thumb was just a strange phrase from an old story. Now it may be a real place in front of him, which means family history may be useful in the present.

QuestionListen For
What does Stanley notice during the storm?He notices a mountain shape that looks like a giant thumb.
How does that connect to his family history?It reminds him of God's Thumb, where his great-grandfather supposedly survived.
Why does this chapter fit the theme of past and present?A family story from the past begins to look connected to Stanley's present setting.

Chapter 30

Teacher — 1 sentence

Mockery over Zero digging for Stanley leads to a fight, and Zero finally refuses to accept humiliation.

Student reader — board word: FIGHT
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

Read-aloud summary — about 2 minutes

Chapter 30 shows the pressure inside the group finally breaking. The other boys keep teasing Stanley because Zero has been digging part of Stanley's hole in exchange for reading lessons. They act as if Stanley thinks he is better than they are, and the teasing becomes cruel.

Mr. Pendanski makes the situation worse instead of helping. He encourages Stanley to fight Zigzag, and the fight quickly becomes dangerous. When Zigzag attacks Stanley, Zero steps in to protect him. The counselors and the Warden arrive, and the boys reveal that Zero has been helping Stanley dig.

The Warden forbids the reading lessons, and Mr. Pendanski insults Zero by calling him stupid. This is the moment when Zero finally refuses to keep accepting humiliation. He hits Mr. Pendanski with a shovel and runs away into the desert. The chapter connects to the past because cruelty again produces violence. Just as injustice shaped Kate Barlow's story, cruelty at Camp Green Lake pushes Zero to a breaking point.

This chapter is also useful for talking about consequences. Stanley and Zero's private deal leads to jealousy. The jealousy leads to teasing. The teasing leads to violence. The adults could stop the chain, but instead they make it worse. Students should notice that the camp's system does not build character; it breaks people down until someone finally explodes.

QuestionListen For
Why are the boys angry about Stanley and Zero's deal?They think Zero digging for Stanley gives Stanley an unfair advantage.
How does Mr. Pendanski make the conflict worse?He encourages fighting and insults Zero instead of calming things down.
What choice does Zero make at the end of the chapter?He refuses to dig more holes, hits Mr. Pendanski, and runs away.

Chapter 31

Teacher — 1 sentence

Stanley worries about Zero while the adults discuss erasing Zero's records as if he never mattered.

Student reader — board word: RECORDS
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

Read-aloud summary — about 2 minutes

Chapter 31 slows down after Zero's escape and lets us see Stanley's guilt. Stanley knows Zero is somewhere in the desert without water. He thinks about trying to help, but every option seems dangerous. If he tells the Warden the truth about where he found the gold tube, X-Ray may turn against him. If he leaves camp, he may die in the desert too.

The adults respond to Zero's disappearance in a chilling way. Instead of acting worried about a missing boy, they talk about changing or erasing his records. They decide that no one will care enough to look for him. This shows how little the camp values Zero as a person.

This chapter is important for Stanley's character. He is not ready to act yet, but he cannot stop thinking about Zero. The present situation also connects to the novel's larger pattern: people with power try to control the story by hiding evidence. Erasing records is another kind of burying the truth.

Students should listen for the difference between Stanley's conscience and the adults' attitude. Stanley feels responsible because he knows Zero is a real person and a friend. The adults speak about paperwork and records. That contrast helps readers understand why Stanley may have to be the one to act, even though he is only a boy and has very little power.

QuestionListen For
Why does Stanley feel guilty?He knows Zero helped him and is now alone in the desert.
What do the adults plan to do with Zero's records?They plan to erase or change them so no one looks for him.
What does this show about Camp Green Lake?The camp treats Zero as if he does not matter.

Chapter 32

Teacher — 1 sentence

Stanley makes a risky choice to steal the water truck and search for Zero.

Student reader — board word: TRUCK
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

Read-aloud summary — about 2 minutes

Chapter 32 introduces a new camper named Brian, whom the boys nickname Twitch because he fidgets and has a habit connected to stealing cars. His arrival makes Zero's absence feel even sharper. Zero's cot is filled, but Stanley knows Zero himself has not been replaced.

Stanley keeps worrying about Zero. When Mr. Sir arrives with the water truck, Stanley sees the keys still in the ignition. He makes a sudden, risky decision: he climbs into the truck and drives away, hoping to rescue Zero. Stanley does not really know how to drive, and almost immediately he crashes the truck into a hole.

Even though the plan fails, this is a major character moment. Stanley is no longer just accepting what happens to him. He chooses to act for someone else, even though the consequences could be terrible. His decision connects to the book's question about fate and choice. Stanley has had bad luck all his life, but here he admits that this mistake is his own choice.

The crash matters because Stanley's courage is not perfect or planned. He makes a messy choice, and it goes badly almost right away. That makes the scene believable. Courage in this chapter does not mean Stanley knows exactly what to do. It means he cannot keep doing nothing while Zero may be dying somewhere beyond the camp.

QuestionListen For
Who is Twitch?He is the new camper who takes Zero's cot.
Why does Stanley steal the truck?He wants to leave camp and find Zero.
How is Stanley changing?He is becoming brave enough to act for a friend, even when it is risky.

Chapter 33

Teacher — 1 sentence

Stanley walks through the dry lake bed and nearly encounters yellow-spotted lizards.

Student reader — board word: LIZARDS
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

Read-aloud summary — about 2 minutes

Chapter 33 follows Stanley after he crashes the truck and runs into the desert. No one follows him immediately, which gives him time to walk away from camp. At first, he tries to make a practical plan. He thinks he might walk halfway to the mountain, then turn back before he becomes too weak.

As Stanley moves across the lake bed, he passes clusters of holes. The holes remind readers of how much labor the boys have been forced to do and how empty the place is. Stanley keeps looking into them, though he does not fully admit what he hopes to find. He is searching for some sign of Zero.

Then he looks into one hole and sees a family of yellow-spotted lizards. This is terrifying because the book has already told us how deadly they are. Stanley runs until he feels safer. The chapter uses the setting to build suspense: heat, thirst, holes, and lizards all show how dangerous Stanley's rescue attempt has become.

The yellow-spotted lizards also remind readers that the desert is full of dangers connected to earlier parts of the book. The holes are not just empty spaces; they are hiding places for deadly creatures. Stanley is crossing a landscape shaped by the Warden's search, the boys' labor, and the old lake's history. Every step makes the rescue attempt feel more dangerous.

QuestionListen For
What plan does Stanley first make?He thinks he can walk partway toward the mountain and then turn back.
What is Stanley really looking for?He is looking for Zero, even if he does not say it clearly to himself.
Why are the lizards so frightening?Yellow-spotted lizards are deadly, and Stanley has no protection.

Chapter 34

Teacher — 1 sentence

Stanley finds Sam's old boat, Mary Lou, and discovers Zero hiding beneath it.

Student reader — board word: MARYLOU
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

Read-aloud summary — about 2 minutes

Chapter 34 brings one of the strongest connections between past and present. Stanley is exhausted and thirsty as he walks toward the thumb-shaped mountain. He sees what looks like water, but it is only a mirage. The desert tricks him, but the mountain still seems to encourage him forward.

Then Stanley notices a large object on the dry lake bed. When he gets closer, he realizes it is an old boat. The name on the boat is Mary Lou, which connects it to Sam, Katherine Barlow, and the old Green Lake timeline. A piece of the past is sitting right in Stanley's present.

Under the boat, Stanley finds a tunnel and then sees an arm in an orange sleeve. Zero is alive. This discovery matters because the past has helped the present: Sam's boat gives Zero shelter. The chapter also rewards Stanley's risky choice. He did not find Zero because the adults cared. He found Zero because he chose friendship.

This chapter is a strong example of an object carrying history. Mary Lou was once Sam's donkey, then the name of his boat, and now the name on the wreck Stanley finds. The boat connects love, loss, and survival. A thing from the old Green Lake story becomes the place where Zero has stayed alive in the present.

QuestionListen For
What object does Stanley find on the lake bed?He finds an old boat named Mary Lou.
Why is the name Mary Lou important?Mary Lou was connected to Sam in the old Green Lake story.
Who does Stanley find under the boat?He finds Zero alive under the boat.

Chapter 35

Teacher — 1 sentence

Zero shares the old jars he has survived on, probably Katherine Barlow's spiced peaches.

Student reader — board word: SPLOOSH
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

Read-aloud summary — about 2 minutes

Chapter 35 shows Stanley and Zero together again under the old boat. Zero is weak and sick, but he refuses to return to camp. He knows the adults there will not protect him, and after what happened with Mr. Pendanski, he has no reason to trust them.

Zero shows Stanley how he has survived. He found old jars filled with a muddy liquid he calls sploosh. Stanley is disgusted at first because the jars are very old, but he is desperately thirsty. When he drinks it, the sploosh tastes like peaches. Readers can connect this to Katherine Barlow's famous spiced peaches from the past timeline.

This chapter is another clear past-present connection. Something made more than a hundred years ago is helping Stanley and Zero survive now. The same place that holds pain and injustice also holds small pieces of rescue. Still, the rescue is incomplete. Zero is sick, they have almost no supplies left, and the mountain is still far away.

The old jars also show how the past can be complicated. Katherine's peaches belonged to a happier part of her life before tragedy changed her. Now those peaches, changed by time into sploosh, help two boys who are suffering because of the Warden's search for Kate's treasure. A sweet thing from the past becomes survival in the present.

QuestionListen For
Why does Zero refuse to return to camp?He does not trust the adults and does not want to be punished again.
What is sploosh?It is an old peach-like liquid Zero found in jars under the boat.
How does sploosh connect past and present?It seems connected to Katherine Barlow's spiced peaches and helps the boys survive.

Chapter 36

Teacher — 1 sentence

Stanley and Zero begin walking toward the mountain while Zero grows weaker.

Student reader — board word: CLIMB
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

Read-aloud summary — about 2 minutes

Chapter 36 begins the boys' attempt to reach the thumb-shaped mountain. Stanley and Zero take what little they have: Zero's shovel, empty jars, the sack with the sunflower seed, and their hope that the mountain might offer water or shelter.

The walk is extremely difficult. Zero has stomach pains, likely from the old sploosh, and he is weak from heat, thirst, and hunger. Stanley tries to help him keep going. He also continues spelling words for Zero, which shows that their friendship and learning have not ended just because they escaped camp.

This chapter shows Stanley growing into the kind of person who carries responsibility. Earlier in the book, he was mostly trying to survive his own punishment. Now he is trying to help Zero survive too. The mountain is important because it connects to Stanley's family past, but reaching it depends on present choices: endurance, loyalty, and not giving up.

This is a good chapter for tracking Stanley and Zero as partners. Stanley has physical strength, Zero has determination, and both boys keep each other moving. Their friendship is now more important than the camp's rules. The mountain still comes from Stanley's family story, but reaching it depends on the boys choosing to trust each other step by step.

QuestionListen For
Where are Stanley and Zero trying to go?They are trying to reach the thumb-shaped mountain.
Why is the trip so difficult?They are weak, thirsty, hot, and Zero has stomach pains.
What does Stanley continue doing for Zero?He keeps helping him and spelling words with him.

Chapter 37

Teacher — 1 sentence

Zero becomes sicker as the boys continue toward God's Thumb.

Student reader — board word: SICK
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

Read-aloud summary — about 2 minutes

Chapter 37 keeps the focus on survival. Stanley and Zero continue toward the mountain, but Zero is getting worse. The heat, lack of water, and old sploosh have taken a serious toll on him. He throws up, and Stanley realizes that their situation is becoming more dangerous.

Even though this chapter is short, it matters because it stretches the tension. The boys are no longer dealing with camp rules or counselor cruelty. They are facing the desert itself. There is no easy rescue, no adult help, and no guarantee that God's Thumb will actually save them.

The chapter also deepens Stanley's loyalty. He could think only about himself, but he keeps paying attention to Zero. Their friendship has become the center of the story. In the past, Elya Yelnats failed to carry Madame Zeroni up the mountain. In the present, Stanley is moving toward a mountain with a member of the Zeroni family beside him, though he does not yet understand the full connection.

Even though little seems to happen on the surface, the chapter increases suspense because Zero's body is failing. Students should notice how the author makes time feel slow. Each step matters. Each delay matters. Stanley is learning that friendship can require endurance, not just one brave moment. Staying with Zero is a continuing choice.

QuestionListen For
What happens to Zero in this chapter?He becomes very sick and throws up.
Why is the desert now the main danger?The boys have little water, little food, and no help.
How does Stanley show loyalty?He stays with Zero and keeps trying to help him move forward.

Chapter 38

Teacher — 1 sentence

Stanley carries Zero up the mountain and finds onions and muddy water near God's Thumb.

Student reader — board word: ONION
Write this word on the board before or after reading the chapter summary.

Read-aloud summary — about 2 minutes

Chapter 38 is one of the most important chapters in this section. Zero becomes too weak to continue, and Stanley makes a major choice. He leaves behind extra supplies and carries Zero up the mountain. This is physically difficult, but it is also symbolically important.

Stanley does not know it yet, but Zero's real last name is Zeroni. Long ago, Stanley's great-great-grandfather failed to carry Madame Zeroni up a mountain as promised. Now Stanley is carrying a Zeroni up a mountain, not because he knows about the curse, but because Zero is his friend. The past and present connect through an act of loyalty.

Near the top, Stanley finds muddy water and onions. The onions connect back to Sam, whose onions were known in the old Green Lake story. The mountain gives the boys a chance to survive. This chapter shows that the past is not only a curse. It also provides healing, food, water, and a way forward.

The onions complete another past-present connection. Sam's onions were once part of the old Green Lake community, and now onions help Stanley and Zero live. The chapter turns old stories into present rescue: God's Thumb, the Zeroni name, the mountain, muddy water, and onions all meet in one place. Stanley does not understand every connection yet, but readers can see the pattern.

QuestionListen For
What does Stanley do when Zero can no longer walk?He carries Zero up the mountain.
Why is that action important for the past-present connection?Stanley carries a Zeroni up a mountain, echoing the old family curse story.
What do the boys find near the top?They find muddy water and onions.
