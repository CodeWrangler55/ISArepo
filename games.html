Literature Class Backup Games

Use these when discussion ends early, students have not read, or the room needs a reset. None require copies of the books.

Rule of thumb: Keep games tied to literature skills: character, setting, conflict, inference, evidence, theme, vocabulary, and point of view.

Fast Picks

Situation Use This Game Time
4th grade has extra energy Character Trait Corners 8-12 min
8th grade is quiet Evidence Court 10-15 min
No one read Conflict Builder 10-15 min
You need a 5-minute filler Vocabulary Lightning 5-8 min
You want students talking Would You Rather: Literature Edition 8-12 min

Works For Both Classes

1. Character Trait Corners

Best for: 4th grade, but usable with 8th grade as a warm-up.

Setup: Label four corners of the room with trait words. Example: brave, kind, careful, determined.

  1. Name a character or situation from the book setup.
  2. Students move to the trait they think fits best.
  3. Each corner gives one reason using: "I chose ___ because ___."
  4. Ask if anyone wants to switch corners after hearing reasons.

Literature skill: Characterization with evidence.

Example: Fern speaks up for Wilbur. Which trait fits best: brave, kind, fair, or determined?

2. Conflict Builder

Best for: both classes when students have not read.

Setup: Write this formula on the board: Somebody wants ___, but ___, so ___.

  1. Give students a simple story seed: "A child finds an animal in danger" or "A family invites a stranger to live with them."
  2. Students complete the formula aloud or on paper.
  3. Share several examples.
  4. Ask which examples create the strongest conflict and why.

Literature skill: Plot and conflict.

Upgrade for 8th: Ask whether the conflict is internal, external, or both.

3. Vocabulary Lightning

Best for: a short filler at the end of class.

  1. Write 5-8 words on the board: protagonist, setting, conflict, theme, inference, evidence, trait, foreshadowing.
  2. Give a definition; students call out the word.
  3. Then reverse it: give a word; students give a definition or example.
  4. End by asking students to use one word in a sentence about the book.

Literature skill: Vocabulary fluency.

4. Would You Rather: Literature Edition

Best for: getting students talking without needing the text.

  1. Ask a choice question.
  2. Students pick a side.
  3. They must defend their answer with one reason.
  4. Connect the reasoning to character, conflict, or theme.

Prompts:

  • Would you rather be a character who has to make a brave choice or a wise choice?
  • Would you rather live in a story set on a farm, by the sea, in a city, or in the wilderness?
  • Would you rather read a story with a happy ending or a surprising ending?
  • Would you rather be the protagonist or the narrator?

Literature skill: Defending an interpretation with a reason.

4th Grade Games

5. Trait Detective

Setup: Write a character action on the board.

Example: "Fern argues that Wilbur should live."

  1. Students list possible trait words.
  2. Pick one trait and say what evidence supports it.
  3. Repeat with a new action.

Sentence frame: "This shows the character is ___ because ___."

6. Story Elements Relay

Setup: Put four headings on the board: character, setting, conflict, theme.

  1. Divide students into two teams or run as one group.
  2. Call out an example: "farm," "Wilbur is in danger," "Fern," "protecting the weak."
  3. Students identify which heading it belongs under.
  4. Ask for one explanation before moving on.

Literature skill: Recognizing story elements.

7. Theme Charades

Setup: Pick simple theme words: kindness, courage, fairness, friendship, responsibility.

  1. One student acts out a theme word silently.
  2. The class guesses the word.
  3. Ask: "How could that theme appear in a story?"

Literature skill: Connecting abstract ideas to story actions.

8th Grade Games

8. Evidence Court

Setup: Put a claim on the board.

Example: "Papa is still grieving." or "Fern is brave."

  1. One side argues the claim is strong.
  2. One side asks for better evidence or a clearer explanation.
  3. Students must use details from the story setup, chapter summary, or class discussion.
  4. You act as judge and ask: "Is that evidence, or just an opinion?"

Literature skill: Claim, evidence, reasoning.

9. Hope vs. Fear

Best for: Sarah, Plain and Tall.

  1. Draw two columns: Hope and Fear.
  2. List characters: Anna, Caleb, Papa/Jacob, Sarah.
  3. Students fill in what each character might hope for and fear.
  4. Ask which character has the most complicated choice.

Literature skill: Motivation, internal conflict, inference.

10. Theme Debate

Setup: Give two possible themes and have students choose which one better fits the story opening.

Examples:

  • Family requires courage.
  • Grief changes a home.
  • Small lives still matter.
  • Speaking up can change a story.
  1. Students choose a theme.
  2. They give one reason.
  3. Ask: "What detail from the story setup supports that?"

Literature skill: Theme and textual support.

11. Point Of View Switch

Setup: Choose one moment from the book setup.

  1. Ask students to retell the moment from another character's point of view.
  2. Keep it short: 2-3 sentences.
  3. Discuss what changes when the narrator changes.

Examples:

  • Retell Fern saving Wilbur from Mr. Arable's point of view.
  • Retell Papa receiving Sarah's letter from Anna's point of view.
  • Retell Sarah deciding whether to visit from Sarah's point of view.

Literature skill: Narrator, perspective, inference.

Closing Line

If a game runs well, close with: "That was not just a game. We were practicing the same thinking good readers use: noticing details, making inferences, and explaining why our answers make sense."
